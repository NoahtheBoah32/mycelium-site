From the Convenor · Baganihan Reports

On an upland site in the Philippines. Photo: Hubert Joseph Balana Posadas.

Permaculture Beyond the Farm: Permanent Culture, Resilience and Security

By Hubert Joseph Balana Posadas, Convenor of The Baganihan Collective

If permaculture also means permanent culture, what has to be inside the design for a community to stay functional under stress? Hubert writes from years of risk management and community security work on Philippine ground. He reads bayanihan as distributed capacity rather than a nice habit, points to the unwritten security practices he has met in barangays, and argues that security may already be part of the culture permaculture is trying to design.

“One does not manufacture Bayanihan. One enters into relationship.”

The argument is his rather than ours. Read it closely, decide what holds, and bring your reading to Common Ground.

 

From the research shelf

Two studies on Philippine permaculture, in numbers.

Dr. Jabez Joshua M. Flores of UP Open University has spent years measuring things most of us only talk about. Two of his papers are open access, and both are going up as carousels on our Instagram this month.

The structure of permaculture landscapes in the Philippines

Flores & Buot · Biodiversitas, 2021

Twelve farms in eleven provinces, surveyed by drone and a one-hectare grid. 1,182 components counted. Perennials made up 75 to 95 percent of species at every site. Their conclusion: these landscapes resemble forests more than farms.

Read the paper 		 

An Emerging Network for Sustainable Agriculture

Flores, Buot, Flor, Bagarinao & Sobremisana · IJSESD, 2023

The first systematic count of practitioners here: 204 people, 75 with a project on the land, 36 with a design certificate. Most do not know each other yet. The authors argue that loose weave is exactly what keeps the network growing.
